# describe before explain

A framework for turning layered observation into a clear sentence in real time.

> The bottleneck is not noticing. It is compression — collapsing many observations,
> patterns, and interpretations into one or two sentences fast enough to say out loud.

---

## the diagnosis

You already have the inputs. Communication gaps at work, provider abstractions
centralizing change, diagrams clarifying systems, product talk drifting off
fundamentals — those are sharp observations. The detail is there.

What costs you the pause is the pipeline you run before speaking:

```
observation -> pattern -> relationship -> context -> interpretation -> speak
```

Most people run `observation -> speak`. You run five layers and then have to
compress the result into a sentence on the spot. That is a different skill, and
it is trainable.

So the target is not "notice more." It is: **separate the layers, then sequence
them out loud** — observation first, interpretation last.

---

## the core move

Lead with what happened. Hold the conclusion until after.

| Don't open with                  | Open with                                                                 |
|----------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| "Scott is gatekeeping."          | "Scott built the project independently; I haven't seen openings for others to contribute." |
| "The meeting was unproductive."  | "The discussion moved off the architecture question and onto product framing." |
| "Nobody understands the system." | "Three people asked the same question about the data flow this week."      |

The interpretation can still follow. But when the observation lands first, the
listener can follow your reasoning instead of reacting to your verdict.

---

## the framework

Three steps. This is the whole thing.

**1. Observe — what literally happened.**
Facts only. No judgment words ("gatekeeping," "unproductive," "broken"). If you
can't say it without a verdict, you haven't found the observation yet.

**2. Pattern — what this is an instance of.**
"This happens when conversations turn product-focused." One sentence. The pattern
is what makes a single event worth mentioning.

**3. Stakes — why it matters.**
"We lose the technical reasoning underneath." This is the payload. If you can't
name the stakes, the observation may not be worth raising.

Said in order, those three become a clean, defensible statement:

> "The discussion moved off the architecture question (observe). That tends to
> happen once we go product-focused (pattern). When it does, we lose the
> technical reasoning underneath (stakes)."

---

## the practice loop

This is the rep. Do it after meetings, 1:1s, or any moment where you felt the pause.

Spend two minutes writing:

```
WHAT HAPPENED   (observation, no verdict)
WHAT PATTERN    (one sentence)
WHY IT MATTERS  (the stakes)
```

You already externalize to think — journaling, vlogging, diagrams, talking to an
AI. That is not a crutch; it is your method. You don't discover your thoughts by
writing, you *organize* them. This loop just makes the organizing deliberate and
repeatable. Over enough reps, you start running the three steps mentally before
you speak, and the pause shrinks.

Anchor every rep to a real situation. Don't practice in the abstract — practice
on the meeting you just left.

---

## validate

How to detect whether the skill is actually landing. Run these as self-checks,
not as grading.

**Per-rep checks** (after each two-minute drill):

- [ ] My observation contains zero judgment words. (If "gatekeeping" or "messy"
      is in it, rewrite it.)
- [ ] My pattern is exactly one sentence.
- [ ] I can compress all three steps into two spoken sentences.
- [ ] A listener could disagree with my interpretation but not with my observation.

**Longitudinal signal** (the real detector):

The thing to watch is not any single rep — it's the trend. Your daily vlogs have
gotten clearer over ~200 days. That is the same metric here. Track it directly:

- Are your post-meeting notes getting *shorter* while staying complete?
- Are you reaching the stakes line faster, with fewer false starts?
- In live conversation, has the pause shortened?

If those three are trending down over weeks, the skill is compounding. If they're
flat, you're collecting observations but not compressing them — go back to the
two-minute loop and force the one-sentence pattern.

---

## failure modes

- **Verdict-first.** You open with the interpretation. Symptom: people react to
  your conclusion instead of your evidence. Fix: demote the verdict to step 3.
- **No stakes.** You state observation + pattern and stop. Symptom: "okay, and?"
  Fix: if you can't name why it matters, it may not need saying.
- **Over-compression.** You skip straight to a one-word label to avoid the pause.
  Symptom: "Scott is gatekeeping." Fix: this framework *is* the alternative.
- **Practicing in the abstract.** Reps not tied to a real event don't transfer.
  Fix: always anchor to something that actually happened today.
